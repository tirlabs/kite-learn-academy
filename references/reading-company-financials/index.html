Reading Company Financials: A Starting Point

A beginner-friendly map to the income statement, balance sheet, cash flow and common financial ratios.

Three connected financial statements

A company's financial statements tell different parts of the same story. The income statement reports performance over a period. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date. The cash flow statement reports cash moving into and out of the business during a period.

Revenue, expenses and profit

Revenue is the income generated from business activities before expenses are deducted. Profit is what remains after the relevant expenses. A company can report higher revenue while profit falls if costs rise faster. Reported profit also does not mean all customers have paid in cash.

Assets, liabilities and equity

Assets are resources the company controls; liabilities are obligations it must meet. Equity is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets. A balance sheet helps readers ask how the business is funded and whether it has resources to meet obligations.

Why cash flow matters

Cash flow helps show whether operations generate cash and how the company funds investment and financing needs. Compare operating cash flow with reported profit across several periods. Persistent differences deserve investigation, but one period alone may not tell the whole story.

A small illustrative example

Imagine a fictional company reports ₹100 lakh in revenue and ₹85 lakh in expenses for a period, giving ₹15 lakh in profit before any further applicable items. If customers have not yet paid ₹20 lakh of invoices, the reported revenue and cash collected will not be identical. This is why profit and cash flow answer different questions.

Ratios are clues, not conclusions

  • EPS: earnings attributable to each share under the stated calculation.
  • P/E: share price compared with earnings per share; interpretation depends on growth, risk, accounting and sector context.
  • ROE / ROCE: measures of returns relative to equity or capital employed; definitions and comparability matter.
  • Working capital: a view of short-term operating resources and obligations.

No single ratio proves that a company is good or a share is cheap. Compare the company's own history, business model, peers, accounting notes and risks. Learn EPS, ROE and ROCE in the curriculum.

Educational information only. Financial statements require context and can contain estimates and accounting judgements. This guide does not provide stock recommendations or buy/sell rules.
