Reading Company Financials: A Starting Point

A beginner-friendly map to the income statement, balance sheet, cash flow and common financial ratios.

Three connected financial statements

A company's financial statements tell different parts of the same story. The income statement reports performance over a period. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date. The cash flow statement reports cash moving into and out of the business during a period.

Revenue, expenses and profit

Revenue is the income generated from business activities before expenses are deducted. Profit is what remains after the relevant expenses. A company can report higher revenue while profit falls if costs rise faster. Reported profit also does not mean all customers have paid in cash.

Assets, liabilities and equity

Assets are resources the company controls; liabilities are obligations it must meet. Equity is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets. A balance sheet helps readers ask how the business is funded and whether it has resources to meet obligations.

Why cash flow matters

Cash flow helps show whether operations generate cash and how the company funds investment and financing needs. Compare operating cash flow with reported profit across several periods. Persistent differences deserve investigation, but one period alone may not tell the whole story.

A small illustrative example

Imagine a fictional company reports ₹100 lakh in revenue and ₹85 lakh in expenses for a period, giving ₹15 lakh in profit before any further applicable items. If customers have not yet paid ₹20 lakh of invoices, the reported revenue and cash collected will not be identical. This is why profit and cash flow answer different questions.

Ratios are clues, not conclusions

  • EPS: earnings attributable to each share under the stated calculation.
  • P/E: share price compared with earnings per share; interpretation depends on growth, risk, accounting and sector context.
  • ROE / ROCE: measures of returns relative to equity or capital employed; definitions and comparability matter.
  • Working capital: a view of short-term operating resources and obligations.

No single ratio proves that a company is good or a share is cheap. Compare the company's own history, business model, peers, accounting notes and risks. Learn EPS, ROE and ROCE in the curriculum.

Where to find reliable company information

For a listed company in India, begin with its official investor-relations website and the filings made to the stock exchanges. Useful documents include annual reports, quarterly or half-yearly results, investor presentations, corporate announcements, shareholding disclosures and notices of material events. A company presentation can be a helpful introduction, but it is prepared by the company and should be read alongside the financial statements, notes and risk disclosures.

Check the reporting period and whether the figures are standalone or consolidated. Standalone statements relate to the individual legal entity. Consolidated statements combine the parent and its subsidiaries under the applicable accounting rules. The right comparison depends on how the business is organised; mixing the two can lead to misleading conclusions.

Understand the three cash-flow categories

The cash flow statement usually groups cash movements into three broad activities:

  • Operating activities: cash generated or used by the company's main business operations.
  • Investing activities: cash used for or received from investments in long-term assets and other investments.
  • Financing activities: cash raised from or returned to lenders and shareholders, such as borrowings, repayments, share issues or dividends.

A growing business may use cash to build factories, buy equipment or expand. Negative investing cash flow is not automatically a warning; ask what the spending is intended to achieve and whether the business can fund it responsibly. Likewise, positive financing cash flow may reflect new borrowing rather than stronger operations.

Read the balance sheet as a relationship

The basic accounting relationship is: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Assets are resources controlled by the business, liabilities are obligations, and equity represents the residual interest. Look at the composition, not just the totals. For example, a large amount of inventory may be normal for one business and a concern for another if stock is not moving. Receivables that grow much faster than sales may deserve a closer look at collections and credit terms.

A worked example: profit and cash are different

Imagine a fictional company reports revenue of ₹100 lakh and expenses of ₹85 lakh during a period. Its simplified profit before other applicable items is ₹15 lakh. Suppose ₹20 lakh of customer invoices remain unpaid at the reporting date. The company may have recognised revenue under the relevant accounting rules even though the cash has not yet arrived. The income statement and cash flow statement therefore answer different questions: one explains reported performance, while the other helps explain cash movement.

Notes, audit reports and comparison periods

Do not stop at the headline numbers. Notes to accounts explain accounting policies, estimates, borrowings, related-party transactions, contingent liabilities and other details. The auditor's report provides an independent opinion on the financial statements and may draw attention to matters that deserve reading. Compare several periods where possible, because one quarter can be affected by seasonality, one-off events or timing differences.

Useful questions include: Is revenue growth translating into operating profit? Is operating cash flow broadly consistent with reported earnings over time? Are debt and interest costs manageable for this business? Are receivables or inventory growing unusually quickly? Are there significant qualifications, uncertainties or unusual transactions in the disclosures?

Common questions

Is revenue the same as profit?

No. Revenue is income from business activities before expenses are deducted. Profit is what remains after the relevant expenses and other items are accounted for.

Can a profitable company have weak cash flow?

Yes. Customers may not have paid yet, inventory may have increased, or cash may be tied up in working capital. Review the cash flow statement and notes to understand why.

Is a high P/E ratio always expensive?

Not by itself. A P/E ratio needs context such as growth expectations, business quality, risk, accounting, interest rates and comparisons with relevant peers. It is a clue for further research, not a conclusion.

Educational information only. Financial statements require context and can contain estimates and accounting judgements. This guide does not provide stock recommendations or buy/sell rules.
