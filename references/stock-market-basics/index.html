Stock Market Basics: How the Market Works

A practical overview of shares, exchanges, brokers and how an order becomes a trade.

What is the stock market?

The stock market is part of the financial system where people and institutions buy and sell shares and other securities. A share represents a unit of ownership in a company. When investors trade shares of listed companies, they are participating in the secondary market.

Think of an organised marketplace where buyers and sellers can meet under common rules. A stock exchange provides the electronic trading platform and processes through which eligible buy and sell orders can be matched.

Why does the stock market exist?

Companies may need money to grow, develop products, build facilities or support their business plans. They can raise capital by issuing securities to investors. Investors, in turn, may buy securities because they want to participate in a company's potential future value or receive other benefits attached to the security.

The market also gives investors a place to trade securities that have already been issued. This can support liquidity and price discovery, although liquidity is not guaranteed for every security.

Primary market and secondary market

In the primary market, an issuer offers new securities to investors. An initial public offering (IPO) is one way a company may offer shares to the public for the first time.

In the secondary market, investors trade securities that already exist. If one investor sells an existing share to another investor, the company does not normally receive the money from that secondary-market trade.

What is a share?

A share is a unit of ownership in a company. If a fictional company has issued 1,000 equal shares and an investor owns 10, that investor owns 1% of those issued shares. This simplified example explains the ownership concept; real companies can have different classes of shares and rights.

Shareholders do not own a specific company asset, and share ownership does not guarantee dividends or profits. The market value of a share can rise or fall.

Who are the main participants?

  • Issuers: Companies and other entities that issue securities.
  • Retail investors: Individuals who invest or trade their own money.
  • Institutional investors: Organisations such as mutual funds, insurers and pension funds that invest under defined mandates.
  • Stockbrokers: Intermediaries that provide investors access to exchanges and related services.
  • Stock exchanges: Institutions that operate trading platforms and market rules.
  • Depositories and participants: Infrastructure that holds securities electronically and provides demat services.
  • Clearing corporations: Institutions that support clearing and settlement after trades.
  • Regulator: SEBI, which regulates India's securities market and works to protect investors.

What are NSE and BSE?

The National Stock Exchange (NSE) and BSE are two principal stock exchanges in India. Investors generally place orders through a broker. The broker routes the order to an exchange's trading system, where it may be matched with a compatible order.

NSE was incorporated in 1992, recognised as a stock exchange in 1993 and began operations in 1994. BSE traces its origins to 1875. NSE and BSE are exchanges; NIFTY 50 and SENSEX are indices associated with those exchanges. An index tracks a selected group of securities and is not itself a marketplace.

What does SEBI do?

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) is the securities-market regulator. Its mandate includes protecting the interests of investors in securities, promoting the development of the market and regulating the securities market. SEBI is not an exchange or a broker.

How does a share trade happen?

  1. Order: An investor selects a security, quantity and order type in a broker's platform.
  2. Routing: The broker routes the order to the relevant exchange.
  3. Matching: The exchange's system matches compatible buy and sell orders under its rules.
  4. Clearing: The clearing process determines the obligations arising from the executed trade.
  5. Settlement: Funds and securities are transferred through the market infrastructure, and the investor's records are updated.

Placing an order is not the same as getting it executed. An executed trade is also not the same as completed settlement. Market procedures and settlement timelines can change, so check current official information when you need operational details.

A simple example: from placing an order to owning shares

Imagine that Anika wants to buy 5 shares of a fictional company called Sunrise Foods. The share is currently being offered by a seller at ₹100 each. Anika enters a buy order for 5 shares through her broker. The broker routes the order to the exchange. If Anika's order matches a compatible sell order under the exchange's rules, the trade is executed for ₹500 before any applicable charges.

  1. Order placed: Anika tells her broker what she wants to buy and the quantity.
  2. Trade matched: The exchange matches her buy order with a seller's compatible sell order.
  3. Clearing: The clearing process works out the funds and securities each side must deliver.
  4. Settlement: Funds and shares are transferred through the market's settlement system according to the applicable cycle.
  5. Demat holding: After settlement, the shares are reflected in Anika's demat account.

This example uses a made-up company and a simplified price to explain the sequence. Real orders may not execute at the displayed price, charges may apply, and an order may remain unfilled or be only partly filled. Buying shares also does not guarantee a profit.

Why do share prices move?

Share prices change as buyers and sellers update the prices at which they are willing to trade. Their decisions may be influenced by company performance, future expectations, industry conditions, economic news, interest rates, global events and investor sentiment. No single factor explains every price movement.

What should a beginner understand before investing?

  • Learn what you are buying and how the product works.
  • Understand the difference between a bank account, trading account and demat account.
  • Know that market prices can fall and that you can lose money.
  • Understand charges, taxes, liquidity and the time horizon relevant to your decision.
  • Use official disclosures and reliable sources; be cautious of tips and guaranteed-return claims.

Frequently asked questions

Does a company receive money every time its shares are traded?

No. A company may receive money when it issues securities in the primary market. In a normal secondary-market trade, one investor buys an existing share from another investor.

Are NSE and BSE the same as stockbrokers?

No. NSE and BSE operate stock exchanges. A stockbroker provides investors access to an exchange and its trading services.

Does placing a buy order mean I own the shares immediately?

Not necessarily. The order must be executed, and the trade must complete settlement. The shares are then reflected in the investor's demat account according to the applicable process.

Can I lose money by investing in shares?

Yes. Share prices can fall, and investors may receive less than they paid. Dividends and profits are not guaranteed.
