Orders and Trading: A Beginner's Guide

Understand market orders, limit orders, the order book and the difference between investing and intraday trading.

From an idea to an order

When an investor decides to buy or sell a listed share, the instruction is entered through a registered broker's trading platform. The order includes the security, quantity and conditions such as order type and price. Placing an order does not mean it has been executed.

The roles of the accounts and intermediaries

A bank account is used for money. A trading account is used to place orders through a broker. A demat account holds securities in electronic form. The broker provides access to the exchange; the exchange's trading system matches eligible buy and sell orders. Learn more about stock brokers and demat accounts.

Market orders

A market order seeks execution at the best available prices. The final price is not guaranteed and can differ from the price last displayed, especially when markets move quickly or liquidity is limited.

Limit orders

A limit order sets the maximum price a buyer is willing to pay or the minimum price a seller is willing to accept. It controls the acceptable price, but execution is not guaranteed: the market may never reach the limit or there may not be enough matching quantity.

Bid, ask and the order book

The bid is the price buyers are currently offering; the ask is the price sellers are currently asking. The difference is the bid–ask spread. The order book displays available orders at different prices. A wide spread or low depth can make execution less predictable.

What happens when orders match?

When compatible buy and sell orders meet under the exchange's matching rules, a trade is executed. Clearing and settlement then arrange the exchange of funds and securities. For the normal equity cash market, NSE describes a T+1 rolling settlement cycle, while an optional T+0 cycle is also available for eligible securities and participants. Actual settlement treatment depends on the segment, security and applicable rules. See NSE's settlement-cycle information.

Before placing an order

  • Check the company/security name and symbol carefully.
  • Confirm buy or sell, quantity and order type.
  • Understand price limits, validity and product type.
  • Review estimated charges and available funds/holdings.
  • Know how your broker handles modification, cancellation and partial execution.

Investing and intraday trading are not the same

Delivery investing generally involves taking delivery of securities, while intraday trading involves positions intended to be opened and closed within the same trading session under the broker's product rules. Intraday trading can involve substantial risk, and broker product labels and square-off rules vary.

Order placement is not the same as execution

An order is an instruction waiting in the exchange system; a trade is the result when a compatible buy and sell order are matched. An order may be fully executed, partly executed, remain pending or be rejected. For example, if you place an order for 20 shares and only 12 shares find matching sellers at the permitted price, the order may be partially filled. The remaining quantity may stay open until it is executed, cancelled or expires under its validity conditions.

Before assuming that a purchase is complete, check the order status and trade confirmation in your broker's platform. An order shown as open or pending does not mean the shares have been bought. After execution, the trade moves through the applicable clearing and settlement process.

Market order versus limit order: a simple comparison

Order typeWhat it prioritisesWhat is not guaranteed
Market orderSeeking execution against available ordersThe exact execution price
Limit orderKeeping the trade within the price limit you specifyWhether the order will execute at all

Imagine a share is displayed near ₹250. A market buy order seeks available sell orders, but the actual average price can be higher if the available quantity is spread across several price levels. A limit buy order at ₹248 will not pay more than ₹248, but it may remain unexecuted if sellers are not willing to sell at that price. These examples are illustrative; quotes and order-book depth change continuously.

How the order book helps explain price

The order book lists buy and sell orders at different price levels. The best bid is the highest displayed buying price, while the best ask is the lowest displayed selling price. The difference is the spread. When a marketable order consumes the available quantity at the best price, it may move to the next price level. This is one reason a large order or a thinly traded security can receive an average execution price different from the last traded price.

Displayed orders can be modified or cancelled before execution, subject to exchange rules. The order book is therefore a live snapshot, not a promise that every displayed quantity will remain available.

Validity, partial fills and order controls

Broker platforms may offer different order-validity choices and product types. The names and availability of these options can vary by broker, exchange segment and security. Read the order preview carefully, including quantity, price, validity, disclosed quantity if applicable, and any trigger conditions. If an order is partly filled, understand what happens to the remaining quantity. If you cancel an order, verify the cancellation status rather than assuming it has taken effect immediately.

Charges and practical risks

A completed trade may involve brokerage and other applicable charges, taxes or statutory levies. The exact amount depends on the transaction, broker plan, exchange segment and current rules. Review the broker's charge schedule and contract note rather than estimating costs from the share price alone. Also remember that a stop-loss instruction cannot guarantee a particular exit price during a fast market, a price gap or limited liquidity.

Common questions

Does placing a buy order mean I own the shares?

No. The order must first be executed and then completed through the applicable settlement process. Check the trade and holdings records in your broker's platform.

Can a limit order remain unexecuted?

Yes. If the market does not reach your limit, or there is insufficient matching quantity, the order may remain open or expire according to its validity.

Is intraday trading the same as delivery investing?

No. They use different product conditions and holding intentions. Intraday positions are generally intended to be closed within the same session, while delivery investing involves taking securities into the demat account after settlement. Each has different risks and broker rules.

Educational information only. This guide explains market mechanics; it is not a recommendation to trade. Order types, charges, settlement rules and tax treatment can change. Verify current details with the exchange, regulator and your broker.
