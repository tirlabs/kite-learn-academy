Mutual Funds and SIPs: A Beginner's Guide

Learn how mutual funds work, understand NAV and expense ratios, compare fund categories and explore SIP investing.

What is a mutual fund?

A mutual fund pools money from investors and invests it according to a scheme's stated objective. The portfolio may include shares, bonds, money-market instruments or other permitted securities. Investors own units in the scheme, and the value of those units changes with the portfolio's value and expenses.

How units and NAV work

Net Asset Value (NAV) is the per-unit value of a scheme, calculated using the value of its assets after deducting liabilities and expenses, divided by the units outstanding. The applicable NAV for a transaction is determined under the scheme's rules and cut-off requirements. A lower NAV does not automatically mean a fund is cheaper or better than another fund.

Common mutual fund categories

  • Equity funds: invest primarily in shares and carry market and company-related risks.
  • Debt funds: invest mainly in bonds and money-market instruments; they can be affected by interest rates, credit quality and liquidity.
  • Hybrid funds: combine different asset classes, such as equity and debt.
  • Index funds: aim to track a chosen index. Their returns may differ from the index because of expenses and tracking difference.

These labels describe broad approaches, not risk-free categories. Read the scheme's stated objective, portfolio, riskometer, costs and disclosures.

What is an expense ratio?

The Total Expense Ratio (TER) represents recurring operating expenses charged to a mutual fund scheme as a percentage of its assets. These expenses affect the scheme's NAV and therefore the investor's outcome. AMFI explains the concept in its expense ratio learning resource. Check the current TER disclosed for the scheme rather than relying on old examples.

What is an SIP?

A Systematic Investment Plan is a method of investing a fixed amount at regular intervals into a mutual fund. Each instalment buys units at the applicable NAV. It can help establish a regular investing habit, but it does not guarantee profit or protect against market losses. Learn more in KiteLearn's SIP lesson and experiment with the SIP calculator.

SIP versus lump-sum investing

An SIP invests money in instalments; a lump-sum investment puts a larger amount to work at one time. The results depend on market performance, timing, fund choice, costs and the investor's circumstances. Neither approach is guaranteed to outperform the other.

Direct and regular plans

Direct and regular plans of the same mutual fund scheme invest in the same underlying portfolio, but the distribution route and expenses can differ. A regular plan typically includes distribution-related costs reflected in its expense ratio; a direct plan does not include distributor commission. Compare the current scheme documents and TER disclosures. Do not choose a plan solely from one headline number without understanding the services and costs involved.

Questions to ask before choosing a scheme

  • What is the scheme's objective and what does it invest in?
  • What risks does the portfolio carry?
  • Does its time horizon fit the purpose of the money?
  • What are the expense ratio and other applicable costs?
  • How has the scheme behaved across different market conditions, and what are the limitations of past performance?
  • Have you read the scheme information document and key information memorandum?
