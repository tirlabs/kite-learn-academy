What is an IPO?

3 mins readLevel: Intermediate

Understand the idea

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is the process through which a company offers shares to public investors for the first time and seeks to list those shares on a stock exchange. It is one way a company can access capital from a wider group of investors.

Think of a growing business that has been owned by a small group of founders. It may decide to offer shares to public investors. After the issue and listing process, eligible shares can generally be bought and sold on the stock exchange.

Where does the money go?

In a fresh issue, the company creates and issues new shares; the proceeds go to the company for the purposes described in its offer documents. In an offer for sale (OFS), existing shareholders sell some of their shares and receive the proceeds. An IPO can include either or both.

What happens during an IPO?

  1. The company prepares offer documents describing its business, financial information, risks and the proposed issue.
  2. The issue opens for applications and investors submit bids through permitted channels.
  3. For applications using ASBA, the application amount is blocked in the bank account rather than transferred immediately. With a valid UPI mandate, the investor authorises the block.
  4. After the issue closes, the basis of allotment is finalised. The amount due for allotted shares is debited; funds for shares not allotted are unblocked.
  5. If the shares are admitted to trading, they can begin trading on the exchange from the listing date.

Does applying guarantee shares or a profit?

No. An application does not guarantee allotment. A listing price can be above or below the issue price, and investors can lose money. Read the offer document, understand the business and risks, and never apply solely because an issue is popular.
